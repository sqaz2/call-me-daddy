This song · Scene study

Cheap to Inform is my idea for a movie scene. I’m writing characters and trying to make the listener feel the pull of each perspective. It is not my family history, and it is not a recording of anyone else’s private conversation.

Personal · Lived experience

Survival Mode comes from my own life. That distinction matters: one is something I’m living through; the other is something I’m trying to understand through a song.

The spark · A note from MusicSubject

A family argument.
An audience on each side.

This isn’t my family story. It’s a scene I imagined after watching a family argument become everybody else’s entertainment.

The starting point was reading about Elon Musk and his daughter, Vivian Jenna Wilson. I kept thinking about the strange position of being a famous person’s child: wanting to be known separately from your parent, while that relationship is the thing strangers keep asking you about.

Then I tried to picture the parent’s side—not to speak for him, but to write a character. Someone who looks at everything they provided and thinks, How can you say I didn’t love you? Across from them is someone thinking, How can you list everything you gave me and still not hear what I needed?

That question doesn’t need a famous surname. A roof, an opportunity, a future somebody worked hard to give you: those things can matter enormously. So can what was missing. I wanted both feelings in the room without pretending one automatically cancels the other.

I don’t have children. I can imagine a parent feeling rejected after believing they gave everything, but I haven’t lived that side of it. Being outside the emotion doesn’t make me objective. It might mean I’m missing the part that matters most.

So I wrote a scene, not a verdict.

The imagined scene

The microphone changes
who they’re talking to.

Voice one · The adult child

“You gave me a roof, then a ceiling to hit.”

Getting out finally means being heard. But the new audience has its own conditions: don’t soften the story, don’t complicate the villain, don’t stop giving us a reason to clap.

Voice two · The parent

“I worked through the nights you remember me gone.”

Providing felt like love. Now being defended by strangers starts to feel like the respect that went missing at home. The story gets easier to tell when their own mistakes stay out of it.

These are fictional characters, not quotations from Musk or Wilson. The turn in the song is when both stop speaking to each other and start speaking to the people cheering them on.

Who buys a ticket
when we sit and fix it?

That is a question inside the scene—not a demand that real people reconcile or abandon a boundary.

The refrain · The crowd becomes the third voice

huge audiences are cheap to mobilize and expensive to inform.

pick a team, punish the other one, feel informed.

By the end, the question reaches the listener too: did I understand something, or did I just enjoy being told I was right?

The real-world starting point Context & sources

In 2022, Vivian Jenna Wilson sought a legal name change and stated that she wanted to distance herself from her father. Her March 2025 Teen Vogue interview discusses that estrangement in her own words.

In July 2024, Musk publicly framed her transition as a loss and said he had been misled about approving treatment. Wilson disputed his account and described him as an absent, unsupportive parent in an NBC News interview (reprint).

Those public accounts prompted the writing; they do not give me access to the whole private relationship. The song is not evidence that either person staged an estrangement, profited from it deliberately, or bears equal responsibility. Its imagined parent is not a claim about Musk’s private motives, and its imagined child is not a claim about Wilson’s.

The focus here is the audience around a conflict—not a judgment on anyone’s gender or an argument that material privilege means somebody owes a parent a relationship.

Leave the scene. Keep the question.

“I came for the truth.
Stayed for the fight.”
