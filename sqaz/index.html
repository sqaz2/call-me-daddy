The actual history

BEFORE THE
ALGORITHMS.

The story did not begin with a 2026 remix, and it did not even begin with a microphone. Sqaz started online first, then the writing and recording followed.

2000–2001 · EARLY ONLINE YEARS

Sqaz existed before the music.

I created the online alias Sqaz around 2000 or 2001. The name came from the number 1 spelled out with keyboard letters. People commonly misspelled it as Squaz or Spaz.

I also built a website teaching people how to hack a game I used to play. I marketed the site myself and, before social media made that kind of reach ordinary, it was generating roughly 1,000 hits a day.

AGE 13–14

Writing before recording.

After my parents locked the computer room door, I was sent to live in an adolescent treatment centre in Halifax, Nova Scotia. That period became a major writing chapter for me: I began writing poetry regularly there, and it was also where I was introduced to drugs and alcohol.

Sqaz already existed as the online identity. What changed here was the habit of turning whatever was in my head into words.

2007 · AGE 17

Sqaz — Kill You.

In high school I recorded Kill You under the name Sqaz — not the Eminem song, my own. I was teaching myself by reading, experimenting and doing the work before video tutorials were the default. It became the first studio-quality rap song I knew of being recorded by someone at my high school.

2010

The hobby turned into a recording education.

I took music business and home recording while I was already composing in FL Studio and learning Pro Tools with an Mbox 2. The point wasn't just rapping. I was interested in how songs were built, recorded and turned into finished things.

2026

I remixed the recording instead of erasing it.

Kill You (Special 2026 version remix from 2007) is the old identity brought back into the present. Not a new threat. Not a cleaned-up autobiography. An old performance put under better lighting.

What we found in it

THE VIOLENCE
WASN'T THE WHOLE SONG.

When we pulled the lyrics apart, the most interesting thing was that the loudest material wasn't necessarily the deepest material.

01
Genre / persona

Horrorcore and shock rap, not a literal confession.

The repeated hook works like a chant of dominance and attention. The violence is theatrical: a persona trying to be impossible to ignore.

02
Underneath the flex

An adolescent power fantasy.

The character solves vulnerability with size, danger and control. That reads less like confidence than a teenager building armor out of performance.

03
Authority

Rebellion is aimed at more than one target.

The sanctuary / “house of God” material isn't just there for shock. It pushes against moral and religious authority — the same instinct that runs through the rest of the song: nobody gets to tell this character where the line is.

04
The meta line

The song admits it is trying to get inside people's heads.

That is the trick in plain sight. The song knows it is provoking the listener. Shock isn't an accidental side effect; it is part of the performance.

05
The weirdly important detail

Then there's Tim Hortons.

A mundane Canadian setting sitting beside horror-movie aggression makes the persona feel even more obviously constructed. Everyday life and theatrical violence crash into each other.

06
The line underneath everything

The song briefly points the fight inward.

Buried inside all the threats is the idea that the real enemy can be internal. The song spends most of its time acting invulnerable, then briefly tells on itself.

Our read
The 17-year-old voice wanted to sound dangerous. The older reading is more interesting: power fantasy, rebellion, fear of vulnerability, performative masculinity — and a kid already writing about the fight with the thing inside himself.
